
DECAY GLOSSARY

All terminology in this document is applicable to chat and document communications. It does NOT denote variable naming necessarily, nor in game dialogue and art. 

Use this as a way to communicate with ELISE about the game or to clarify text within the document.


________________



DECAY
1. The name of the game and project

2. the process that causes a dice to be unavailable until the current game is over, removes the dice instance from the board while playing the designated animation, and breaks the slot beneath that dice. 

3. The Label on the Hourglass during the reposition phase, which designates to the player what will happen when they push it -that being the decay process. 


DICE
An in game object that represents a dice. By default this has 6 faces with values 1- 6 on these faces. Each dice face has a roll value. Dice faces also have score values separate from their roll value. Dice may also have a general score value that applies to all faces on the dice. Dice can have effects. Dice faces can be altered.

SLOT
A position on the board where a dice is placed. Each side has 6 opposing slots and can only place dice in the slot designated for their side. A slot can be unbroken, unstable, or broken. Slots all become unbroken when a game ends. Slots are labeled 1E 2E 3E 4E 5E 6E for enemy and 1P 2P 3P 4P 5P 6P for player. Slots process in order from 1 to 6, and a slot with the same value for enemy and player will process at the same time: ie, slot 1E and 1P, then slot 2E and 2P.

ROLL
1. The label on the hourglass during the setup phase that designates to the player what will happen when it is pushed -that being rolling the dice.

2. The process of all dice in board slots selecting a random face, playing the roll animation, and then ending on the correct sprite for the selected face. 

BOARD
The area in the scene where the dice slots and slot labels exist. The board can have dice dropped into its slots from the inventory, or populated to its slots by the enemy. 

ENEMY
The name for the AI opposing the player in the battle. 

PLAYER
The name for the one controlling the interface during the battle

ROLL VALUE
A value between 1 and 6 that is used for the DECAY PROCESS and corresponds to a dice face

SCORE VALUE
A value assigned either to a dice or a dice face. By default a dice has a score value of 0 and each dice face has a score value equal to its roll value. This value can be altered within a battle temporarily. A dice's score value and its face's score_value are separate but both are added to the score when the dices score is processed.

FACE
One of the states a dice can have that refers to a specific roll value and sprite -if a dice has score value associated with a specific face it also refers to this.

OPPOSING
Used to denote two slots with the same numeric value across from each other. I.e. 1E and 1P are opposing. 1E and 2P are not opposing.

SIDE
A set of slots belonging to exclusively the player or the enemy. Ie. 1E 2E 3E 4E 5E 6E is a side. The terms Players side and Enemies side are common.

BROKEN
The state of a slot after a dice inside of it has decayed or after an unstable slot is finished scoring. A slot that is broken displays the appropriate sprite and cannot have any dice placed into it until the game is over. If all slots are broken on either side the game is over automatically after the scoring process is done.

UNSTABLE
The state of a slot after a dice inside it has been SAVED. The slot becomes unstable, and after the score process ends it breaks. The dice in this slot is not disabled for the remainder of the game, and is not reset. Instead, it goes back to the battle inventory.

UNBROKEN
The state of a slot where a dice can be placed into it and that dice will score, roll and can be swapped during the reposition and setup phase


RESET
When the accumulated states, values, and data of a version of an object are changed back to their initial state. With dice this means the state recorded inside the global inventory. With objects this means whatever state is designated by their behavior


SWAP
When two objects (dice) exchange positions on the board. I.e. dice A in 1E swaps with the dice B in 3E. After the swap dice B is in 1E and dice A is in 3E. Dice cannot swap with opposing sides dice. Dice can swap with inventory dice if the battle is in the setup phase.

SETUP
The initial phase of each round. During this phase the hourglass displays ROLL, and the slots on the board are available to have dice added to them if they are not broken. This phase happens after scoring and denotes the beginning of a round.

REPOSITION
The phase after the dice have been rolled. The hourglass displays DECAY. Dice can be swapped between their sides slots but cannot be swapped into the inventory. When the hourglass is selected again it begins the decay, then the scoring process before finally returning the battle to the setup phase

PROCESS
The term for a series of actions performed in a specific order that are shown to the player visibly. The DECAY process and the SCORE process are the main two, but the INVENTORY MOVEMENT process and others will be added. The Decay process always happens before the Score process.

DECAY PROCESS
The series of resolutions from slot 1 to 6 in order that check for roll values of 1 and 6, and act accordingly to the decay rules.

SCORE PROCESS
The process of  dice adding their score value to the ABACUS in order from 1 to 6, score value is separate for enemy and player sides. 

EFFECT
A specific action that a dice performs when it is processed that alters the score value, decay, save or other aspects of DECAY. These are dice specific and not available during the tutorial

CHARM
An addition that causes a effect, but this effect is enabled the entire battle.

HOURGLASS
The in game object that designates round phase changes and can be clicked by the player to start these.

ABACUS
The in game object that record the player and enemy scores

DRAWER
The in-game visual interface that holds the player’s currently available Battle Inventory dice that can be interacted with and placed on the board during the Setup Phase. It also displays information about selected dice.

ROUND COUNTER
The in game object shown as a yellow hand that displays what round is active

BATTLE
A series of 2 games.

GAME
A series of 4 rounds.

RESOLVED
A state meaning an object or slot has completed all actions and rule checks required of it during the current process, and its result is final for that process. Once resolved, later objects or slots cannot change that result unless a rule specifically says otherwise.

ROUND
One process of setup phase, roll, reposition phase, decay process, score process, and a reset.

ROUND SCORE
The score value of  a sides dice after the decay process has resolved all added together. This is added to the total score

TOTAL SCORE
The score displayed on the abacus that persists between rounds and games. The total of all of a sides round scores.

BATTLE INVENTORY
The collection of dice available to one side during a battle. Dice can be moved from here to the board during the setup phase . this inventory is populated at the start of the battle, and contains the battle-specific versions of dice, including any temporary changes made during play. If a dice is decayed then its battle inventory information resets and it should be repopulated from the global inventory.Changes made to dice in the Battle Inventory do not alter the Global Inventory unless specifically stated.



GLOBAL INVENTORY
The source of truth for dice data, including faces, roll values, effects, and score values. This is not edited by battle changes to these values unless this is specifically denoted. If a dice decays then its data is repopulated from this inventory after the game is over rather than from the in battle data.


SELECT
 The action of choosing an interactable object as the player’s current focus. A selected object may display selection feedback or allow actions associated with selection.
DESELECT
 The action of removing the current selection from an object. After being deselected, the object is no longer the player’s current focus.
Use unselected when describing the state itself.
AVAILABLE
 A gameplay state meaning a dice or interactable object is currently allowed to receive the player interactions permitted by the current phase. Availability does not describe whether a slot is broken or whether an object is selected.
UNAVAILABLE
 A gameplay state meaning a dice or interactable object currently cannot receive player interaction. It may still exist visibly and participate in game processes.
PROCESSING
 A temporary state meaning an object is currently being handled by a game process and cannot be interacted with by the player until that processing is complete.

WILLDECAY
 A dice with a roll value of 6. It is a source of decay and will itself decay during the Decay Process unless it is successfully saved.
TARGETED
 A dice opposing a WILLDECAY dice. A targeted dice is threatened with decay from that opposing 6 unless it is successfully saved. Being targeted does not mean decay has already occurred.
DECAYED
 A dice whose decay has completed. It is removed from the board, becomes unavailable for the remainder of the current game, and causes its slot to become broken.
SAVE
The action of preventing one eligible dice from decaying. A save becomes available when a dice with a roll value of 1 is processed during the Decay Process. It can only protect an eligible dice in a later slot on the same side.



WILLSAVE
A dice with a roll value of 1 that has already been processed during the Decay Process and has an unused save available. It will protect the first eligible later dice on its own side that would otherwise decay.


SAVIOR
 A 1 that has already used its willsave to protect another dice. Its save has been consumed and it cannot save another dice that round.
PROTECTED
 A descriptive term for a dice whose pending decay is currently being prevented by a save. This describes what the save is doing to the dice, rather than a separate long-term state.

SAVED
A dice that would have decayed during the current Decay Process but was successfully protected by a save. A saved dice is allowed to score, while its slot becomes unstable and breaks after scoring.






DECAY RULES
DECAY CORE LOOP
* There is a single board with 12 slots, 6 hold the players dice and 6 hold the enemy dice
* Player and enemy place dice from their inventory into their board slots. Placement phase
* Player presses the hourglass to roll the dice
* The dice are rolled,
*  the enemy repositions their dice based on strategy, and then reposition phase begins for the player
*  Then player observes the enemies position and repositions their dice
* The player then presses the hourglass again to activate decay, 
* Decay and saves are processed. Refer to decay rules.
*  the remaining dice after decay are scored 
* The enemy score is added to their game score, and the player round score is added to their game score.
* After score process, any unstable slots break and return their dice to designated inventories. The dice remaining on the board return to available, and the player is able to place or swap out dice in their inventory
* They continue for 4 rounds or until all slots on one side are broken.
* After this, the broken slots reset, the round counter resets, and dice decayed during the game are repopulated to the inventory. The game score is added to the battle score, and then resets.
   * Decayed dice that are repopulated to the inventory should be repupoulated from the GLOBAL inventory -meaning and buffs or debuffs received during a game are reset after decay.
* 4 round/total board break is one game. The player can then start another game.
* After 2 games the total scores are compared to determine the winner
DECAY RULES
* When a dice has a roll value of 6, this means it will decay
   * Decay means the dice with the roll value 6 and the dice in the opposing slot will be destroyed in the decay phase and therefore will not score. 
   * Any slot containing a dice that decays will break, and cannot be used again until the board is reset
   * If a slot containing a dice is saved, it will become unstable and will break after that rounds scoring is completed. 
   * When an unstable slot breaks it does not decay the dice, but instead returns it to the designated inventory.
   * Decay activates in order from slot 1 to 6, enemy and player slots are simultaneous
* When a dice has a roll value of 1 it acts is a save
   * A save is activated after its slot is resolved. This means slot 1 will never be saved.
   * A single save can negate the effect of a single decay on a dice occuring in a later slot on its own side.. 
   * Saves are counted separately between enemy and player
   * A single willsave negates a single decay on a dice, this happens in order of activation (eg, if a dice is already a savior, it cannot save another dice from decaying)
   * willsave protects the first eligible later decay belonging to its own side, then is consumed.
   * If a 1 is opposing a 6 it will decay, and is not counted as a save
   * Willsave resets at the end of each round
* Decay and score resolve from left to right, slots 1 to 6
GAMBLE: 
*  each side has a gamble. 
* - To use a gamble, a player can select a dice from their own side by clicking it once, and then press a button (placeholder)
* This button will reroll the selected dice once. It cannot be rerolled again.
* Each side gets a single gamble per game.
* A gamble can only select dice on the designated owners side

UNSTABLE: 
   * when an unbroken slot is opposing a broken slot, it becomes unstable.
   * Unstable slots do not decay the dice held in them
   * Unstable slots break after the scoring process is over.
   * Unstable slots put any dice contained inside them when broken back to the designated inventory, and they do not reset the dices gained score/effects from the battle


EDGE CASE
If a 6 opposes a 6 both will decays
Scenario: the player side has a willsave in slot 1. and the enemy side does not have a willsave in slot 1. In slot 2 both sides have a 6 . the player 6 in slot 2 is saved from decay, the willsave in slot 1 becomes a savior. The enemy 6 in slot 2 decays.

If a 6 opposes a 6 with an available save, there is no “double decay” case. The 6 is saved normally.


   * Enemy always places dice and repositions dice prior to the player, in order to allow the player to strategize around these actions. Think slay the spire enemies or inscyption
   * Dice cannot be switched out of the board during the reposition phase, they can only be swapped between unbroken slots
   * Dice have roll value and score value in code. This means that a dice with a roll of 6 can have a score value of higher than 6 but will still decay normally
   * If all slots on either side are broken, the game is over and the board resets after both processes have completed.
   * If there is a 1 v  6, the 6 decays the 1 and the 1 does not count as a save
   * If a 1 is activated after a 6 has decayed, the one will not save the decayed 6 (willsave is only possible after a slot has activated)
   * Players or enemies can choose to leave a board slot empty -though it is not recommended
   * battle inventory is 10, but global is larger. so a player can own more than 10 dice in total, but can only use 10 per battle. if a player only owns 8 total dice then they will not use all of their inventory space.
KEY STRATEGY IDEAS
   * Values of 6 and 1. 
   * 6 is highest base score value but only if it is saved
   * 1 has little score value, but can protect higher values
   * 6 can be used offensively to stop opposing high score values
   * 5 is the highest non-decaying roll
   * Targeting
   * Targeting opposing saves in order to stop them from activating
   * Targeting the highest possible score value
   * Slot 1
   * Vulnerable as anything in this spot cannot be saved
   * If a savior is in slot 1 it can be very powerful
   * Generally willsave more useful in slots 1-3
   * Slot 6 
   * A willsave in slot 6 has is bad, it cannot ever become a savior
   * High score value dice in slots 3-6 increases chances of them scoring, as these slots are more likely to be saved
   * Mutual destruction
   * If positioning restricts willsave placements, or the opposing roll has a large amount of willsaves or decays, the best strategy is to keep the slot numbers even if possible
   * If ahead in score and have large amount of decays available, sometimes allowing willsaves to go unused is the best option
   * Priority of score over decay mechanics
   * Sometimes decaying a willsave is worthwhile as it has a low score value
   * Sometimes decaying opponents highest values over their saves is worthwhile -later rounds
Board changes
   * The board naturally shrinks inward as the game progresses. Slot 1 is often first to break. After slot 1 breaks, slot 2 inherits the unique characteristics that slot 1 had. This affects how slots should be considered











DECAY CODE BEST PRACTICES
PURPOSE
This document is the central technical source of truth for rebuilding DECAY in Unity. It records what each system owns, how systems may communicate, which behavior must remain faithful to DECAY, and which responsibilities must be separated during the rewrite.




AUTHORITY ORDER
1. Elise's DECAY GLOSSARY and DECAY RULES.
2. Architecture decisions explicitly reviewed and approved by Elise in this document.
3. The current DECAY_v8_21 GameMaker build for behavior that is already implemented and has not been intentionally changed.
4. CardHouse and spire-codex as design references only.
5. Temporary implementation choices made while programming.


During migration, DECAY_v8_21 is the fallback evidence for existing behavior. After the Unity rebuild is established and verified, the current Unity project, its tests, and its recorded behavior will replace GameMaker as the evidence for what is implemented. Existing code never automatically outranks the DECAY RULES or Elise's confirmed decisions merely because it has already been implemented.




REBUILD
The Unity version is a rebuild, not a line-by-line translation. Existing GameMaker behavior should be preserved when it represents intended DECAY behavior, but existing ownership mistakes, mixed responsibilities, hardcoded screen assumptions, and prototype-only visuals should not be reproduced.




RULES SECTION BOUNDARY
The DECAY RULES section belongs to Elise. Architecture sections may describe the flow needed to execute those rules, but must not silently create or alter gameplay rules. If a technical section conflicts with the completed rules, the rules win and the technical section must be corrected.




DECAY CODE BEST PRACTICES




1. ONE AUTHORITATIVE OWNER PER FACT
Every mutable fact must have one owner. Other systems may read it or request a change, but they must not maintain competing copies.


Examples:
- BattleState owns the current phase value; the hourglass only displays it.
- BoardState owns slot occupancy; a DiceView's transform does not prove which slot it occupies.
- GlobalInventoryState owns permanent dice data; BattleInventoryState owns battle-local mutations.
- ScoreState owns score totals; the abacus text and beads only display them.

2. DEFINITIONS ARE IMMUTABLE DURING PLAY
DiceDefinition, CharmDefinition, EnemyDefinition, EffectDefinition, and configuration assets are authored in the editor and treated as read-only at runtime. Runtime copies contain changes.


3. VIEWS DO NOT ENFORCE RULES
DiceView, SlotView, HourglassView, AbacusView, DrawerView, and EnemyView display state and collect input. They send requests to controllers. They do not alter scores, phase, occupancy, inventory, saves, or decay results directly.


4. USE STABLE DEFINITION IDS AND UNIQUE INSTANCE IDS
Every permanent content definition must have an explicit stable ID, such as die.clockwork, charm.king_chess_piece, or enemy.tutorial_jester. The stable ID must not depend on a display name, list position, scene instance ID, filename, or prefab reference.


Every individually owned dice must also receive a mandatory unique instance ID. Two owned Clockwork Dice share the same definition ID but have different instance IDs. Permanent inventory data and save data use the instance ID to distinguish individual owned copies and the definition ID to identify what each copy is.


Display names, art, asset locations, and prefabs may change without changing either identity stored in existing save data.


5. USE ENUMS AND SMALL VALUE TYPES FOR STRUCTURAL CONCEPTS
Use typed values for BattlePhase, Side, SlotCondition, InteractionState, ProcessType, EffectTrigger, and PresentationChannel. Use a SlotId containing Side plus index rather than passing strings such as "1P" throughout rule code.


This rule does not prefer hardcoded gameplay values or raw indexes. It replaces unclear strings and numbers with values that state what they mean. For example, use Side.Player instead of 0, BattlePhase.DecayProcess instead of "decay", and SlotId(Side.Player, 3) instead of "3P".


Do not use enums for growing content catalogs such as individual dice, charms, enemies, or effects. Those use stable definition IDs and editor-authored definition assets.


6. KEEP RULE CALCULATION IN PLAIN C# WHERE PRACTICAL
Decay, scoring, movement validation, enemy planning, and other rule decisions should be executable without requiring a loaded Unity scene. Plain C# rule classes read authoritative runtime state and editor-authored configuration, then return decisions or approved operations. MonoBehaviours connect scene objects, input, Unity lifecycle, and presentation to that rule code; they do not contain every rule themselves.


For example, a SlotView may detect a dropped dice and send a MoveDiceRequest. The board's rule code decides whether the move is legal. The view then displays the accepted or rejected result.


This does not require an in-game test harness and does not prevent Inspector configuration. It prevents core gameplay from becoming trapped inside scene objects, animations, or Update methods.

7. DO NOT USE UPDATE FOR WORK THAT IS EVENT-DRIVEN
Use facts, explicit method calls, coroutines, tasks, or controlled process execution for phase changes, score updates, selection, slot changes, completed animations, and other discrete changes. A system changes an authoritative fact once and reports that completed change once.


Update, LateUpdate, and FixedUpdate are reserved for genuinely continuous behavior such as an active drag following the pointer, continuous camera motion, or physics. Phase progression and rule outcomes must never depend on a condition eventually being noticed during a frame loop.


8. NO HIDDEN SCENE SEARCH AS NORMAL COMMUNICATION
Do not use FindObjectOfType, GameObject.Find, string object names, or untracked global singletons as routine communication between systems. Use serialized references, construction or setup references, or a documented scene composition root.


9. NAMES MUST IDENTIFY ROLE; REQUESTS ASK, COMMANDS DIRECT, AND FACTS REPORT
Names must identify what type of thing a script, object, method, or data record represents. Generic variations such as PhaseChange, PhaseCheck, or PhaseCurrent are not sufficiently specific when many phase-related systems exist.


Use names such as:
- PhaseChangeRequest for an attempt to change the phase.
- PhaseTransitionValidator for the rule that determines whether a transition is legal.
- AdvancePhaseCommand for an approved operation.
- PhaseChangedFact for a record that the authoritative change occurred.
- CurrentBattlePhase for the value currently stored by the owner.
- BattlePhaseController for the system responsible for phase progression.


A Request asks an authority to consider a change. A Command describes an approved operation to execute. A Fact reports something that has already occurred. Facts are normally created only after authoritative state has changed. Presentation messages must identify presentation explicitly, such as DecayPresentationStartedFact.


Fact does not need to be added mechanically to every ordinary C# event name when the event's payload and role are already explicit. Specificity is mandatory; one particular suffix is not.

10. EFFECTS PRODUCE APPROVED OPERATIONS
Dice and charm effects may inspect an EffectContext and produce approved commands or modifiers. They may not advance phases, directly move views, or freely rewrite unrelated state.


11. ORDER MUST BE EXPLICIT AND DETERMINISTIC
Resolution order, effect order, save consumption, enemy decisions, and random rolls must not depend on Unity hierarchy order, object creation order, collection iteration order, or frame timing.


12. RANDOMNESS COMES FROM ONE INJECTED SOURCE
Normal battles use a seeded random source. Tutorials may use a scripted source. Dice, enemies, and effects must not make scattered direct calls to UnityEngine.Random.


13. EDITOR-EXPOSED CONFIGURATION PROVIDES DATA; RULE CODE INTERPRETS IT
Important values that Elise may need to adjust must be explicitly available in the Unity editor rather than buried inside code. This includes both presentation values and gameplay configuration such as animation speed, easing, spacing, sound, colors, offsets, round limits, battle inventory capacity, dice-face values, effect amounts, enemy decision weights, and tutorial roll sequences.


Rule code defines what each value means and applies it consistently. For example, the editor stores the configured round limit; the Battle Flow rules determine when that limit ends a game. Changing an algorithm or gameplay relationship requires an explicit rule change rather than hiding alternate rule ownership in a view or prefab.


Related configuration must be grouped into clearly named assets or components with visible labels, tooltips, validation, and intentional defaults. Important values must not be scattered across unrelated scene objects. Definition assets store authored configuration only and never live mutable battle state.


14. PRESENTATION MAY DELAY FLOW BUT MAY NOT CONTROL RULE RESULTS
Rules determine what happens. Presentation shows that result and reports when a required animation, sound, or movement has finished. The executor may wait for a blocking presentation before beginning the next approved operation, but the presentation never decides whether a dice decays, scores, moves, saves, or changes a slot.


Blocking presentation completes through a real completion signal rather than a duration guessed by rule code. State changes occur at explicit command boundaries or explicit presentation cues controlled by the executor. An animation event may report that its visual impact point was reached; it may not independently create, cancel, or reverse the gameplay result.


If a presentation is skipped, cancelled, disabled, or interrupted by leaving the scene, the view must reconcile to the authoritative state. Presentation failure must not corrupt rules, advance a process twice, or leave required gameplay permanently blocked.


15. CANCEL CLEANLY
Scene unload, battle exit, restart, skipped tutorial dialogue, and interrupted presentation must cancel pending presentations and commands without leaving state half-committed. Each command has an explicit point before which it may be abandoned and an explicit point after which its completed state must be preserved and displayed correctly.


16. DESIGN FOR KNOWN REQUIREMENTS; AVOID SPECULATIVE SYSTEMS AND DISPOSABLE FOUNDATIONS
Build reusable foundations for DECAY's confirmed scope and known future needs. Do not implement a drastically simplified temporary version when established requirements already show that the foundation must support more.


Known requirements include replaceable random sources for tutorials, definition and runtime separation for dice effects, unique identity for owned dice, editor-tunable configuration, ordered blocking presentation, reusable board movement authority, a looped inventory carousel, and future expansion beyond the tutorial.


Do not build unrelated general-purpose frameworks for hypothetical needs. Do not omit known requirements merely because a narrower implementation is faster at the current moment. Use common effect definitions and commands where behavior is shared, and use a specific implementation where content genuinely requires unique behavior.




17. DO NOT SILENTLY OVERRIDE BEHAVIOR OR ENCODE UNKNOWN DECISIONS
Stop and ask Elise before implementation when:
- A requested edit conflicts with the DECAY RULES or another confirmed function.
- A new behavior would replace, supersede, or remove an implemented behavior.
- The current GameMaker behavior and this document disagree.
- Two sections assign authority over the same fact to different systems.
- A change affects permanent inventory data, save compatibility, gameplay order, or player-visible behavior in an unclear way.
- A temporary visual request would require a lasting architectural compromise.
- A system must be substantially rebuilt or manufactured from scratch.


Before designing or rebuilding a system, first inspect the DECAY RULES, this document, the current implementation, and the relevant CardHouse or spire-codex reference. Use those references to propose a structure designed for long-term DECAY use. If that structure changes or expands existing behavior, present the conflict and ask before implementing it.


Resolved decisions must be added to this document so they do not remain hidden in code or require the same question to be answered repeatedly.




18. BUILD A SMALL STRUCTURED BATTLE HISTORY; KEEP DIAGNOSTICS OUTSIDE GAMEPLAY AUTHORITY
Create a small BattleHistory foundation from the beginning. BattleHistory records an ordered sequence of resolved Facts such as DiceRolledFact, DiceMovedFact, SaveCreatedFact, SaveSpentFact, DiceSavedFact, DiceDecayedFact, SlotBrokenFact, ScoreAppliedFact, RoundEndedFact, GameEndedFact, and BattleEndedFact.


Each Fact uses the stable identities and context needed to understand it, including an ordered sequence number and relevant battle, game, round, side, slot, source, target, and value information. Begin with only the Facts required by implemented systems, but use a structure that can accept additional Fact types without being rebuilt or distributed across unrelated systems later.


BattleState remains the authority for what is true now. BattleHistory records what has already resolved. If gameplay needs a current fact such as whether a dice has scored this round, the appropriate gameplay state or tracker owns it rather than reconstructing it from a text log.


Developer logging converts structured Facts into readable diagnostic messages. Simulations and metrics observe the same rule results and Facts. Debug controls and developer cheats submit the same approved requests as the game. Logging, simulation, metrics, and debug tools do not become required owners of battle rules or state.






DECAY CODE DO AND DO NOT CHECKLIST




CODE DO
1. Give every script, object, method, field, request, command, Fact, and configuration asset a specific name that communicates its DECAY responsibility and role.
2. Keep one authoritative validation path for each rule.
3. Consolidate shared behavior into focused helper functions or shared rule operations.
4. Reuse one underlying rule implementation when player actions, enemy actions, tutorials, simulations, or effects need the same behavior.
5. Cache required component and object references during setup.
6. Expose important adjustable values through clearly organized editor configuration.
7. Use elapsed time correctly for continuous motion and the fixed timestep for physics.
8. Keep frame methods small, predictable, and limited to genuinely continuous work.
9. Use comments to explain non-obvious DECAY rules, ordering requirements, ownership restrictions, and the reason behind unusual decisions.
10. Cancel pending work and unsubscribe listeners when their owner or scene ends.
11. Report broken editor configuration and invalid state clearly.




CODE DO NOT
1. Do not repeat the same validation checks across multiple systems.
2. Do not create wrappers, managers, services, or forwarding methods that add no meaningful responsibility.
3. Do not use generic names such as Helper, Utils, Data, Handler, Thing, Object, or GameManager.
4. Do not leave long blocks of repetitive code when one focused helper or shared operation can express the behavior.
5. Do not maintain multiple functions that perform the same rule or state change.
6. Do not make gameplay logic, rule outcomes, timers, movement speed, or process order dependent on frame rate.
7. Do not call GetComponent, FindObjectOfType, GameObject.Find, or hierarchy searches inside loops or frame methods.
8. Do not perform unnecessary allocations or heavy work inside Update, LateUpdate, or FixedUpdate.
9. Do not repeatedly construct temporary lists, concatenate diagnostic strings, run unnecessary LINQ queries, instantiate and destroy objects, or scan collections every frame.
10. Do not over-comment code or write comments that merely narrate the next line.
11. Do not store live runtime state in definition ScriptableObjects.
12. Do not use strings, magic numbers, hierarchy order, or list position to represent structured gameplay concepts or permanent identity.
13. Do not directly mutate state or collections owned by another system.
14. Do not silently catch, ignore, or conceal errors.
15. Do not leave event listeners subscribed after their owner ends.
16. Do not leave coroutines, tasks, animation waits, commands, or presentation handles running after cancellation or scene exit.
17. Do not expose mutable fields publicly. Use private serialized fields and owner-controlled methods.
18. Do not bury important editable values inside code.
19. Do not scatter related configuration across unrelated objects or prefabs.
20. Do not implement a temporary simplified foundation that ignores a confirmed future requirement.
21. Do not manufacture a new system from scratch without first reviewing the relevant DECAY implementation and reference architecture.
DECAY UNITY INTEGATION NOTES
UNITY INTEGRATION NOTES
This section records how DECAY's established rules and architecture connect to Unity systems. It does not redefine battle phases, process order, gameplay rules, or the general code practices established elsewhere in this document.




REQUEST, GATE, COMMAND, FACT, AND PRESENTATION FLOW
Player input, enemy planning, tutorial direction, effects, and developer tools must use the same controlled route into authoritative gameplay systems.


Standard flow:


Player, AI, tutorial, effect, or developer tool creates a Request
    -> the owning controller evaluates the relevant Gate collection
    -> the controller or resolver approves an outcome
    -> one or more Commands perform the approved state changes
    -> completed changes produce structured Facts
    -> presentation displays those Facts or Command results
    -> required blocking presentation reports completion
    -> the executor continues to the next approved operation


A Request asks for an action. It does not guarantee success.


A Gate answers one narrow permission question by reading authoritative state. It does not change state or perform the requested action. Related Gates are composed into one documented validation path for that interaction, following the useful structure demonstrated by CardHouse's Gate architecture.


For example, a MoveDiceRequest may be evaluated by Gates that determine:
- Whether movement is permitted during the current phase.
- Whether the acting side controls the dice.
- Whether the dice's current location permits movement.
- Whether the requested destination is legal.
- Whether the dice is currently unavailable because it is participating in a process.
- Whether a tutorial restriction temporarily blocks the move.
- Whether another blocking operation prevents interaction.


A rejected Gate returns a specific denial reason that presentation may use for feedback. Views, input scripts, enemy logic, and tutorials must not recreate separate versions of the same checks.


A Command is an approved operation. A Fact records a completed authoritative change. Presentation displays the result and may delay the next operation until a required visual completes, but it does not decide whether the state change is valid




INTERACTION AVAILABILITY, PROCESSING, AND ANIMATION
Interaction availability, processing status, and animation state answer different questions and must not be stored as one combined state.


INTERACTION AVAILABILITY
Interaction availability answers whether a particular player interaction is currently permitted: Available or Unavailable. Availability may differ by interaction. A dice could be available for inspection while unavailable for movement.


Availability is determined through the relevant Gate collection using authoritative state. It must not be decided independently by a sprite, button, collider, or animation.


PROCESSING STATUS
Processing describes whether an object is currently participating in an executing gameplay process or Command. Processing is not a third interaction state. An object that is Processing is unavailable for interactions that could interfere with that process, but an Unavailable object is not necessarily Processing.


The active process or executor is authoritative over processing participation. Do not create competing processing flags on several views and controllers when that status can be read from the executing process.


ANIMATION STATE
Animation state answers what the view is currently displaying: Idle, Hovered, Selected, Rolling, Moving, Decaying, Scoring, and other presentation states. Animation state does not establish interaction permission or gameplay status.


Example during decay:
- Move interaction: Unavailable.
- Processing status: Participating in Decay Process.
- Animation state: Decaying.


A dice may be Unavailable while visually Idle. It may also participate in one process while moving through several animation states.







EDITOR-FIRST PRESENTATION
Favor Unity's editor-authored systems for visual and audio presentation. Use animation clips, Animator states, animation curves, sprite changes, transform animation, prefabs, audio references, visual-effect assets, serialized presentation settings, and animation events rather than recreating those behaviors through hardcoded visual sequences.


Code requests a named presentation, supplies its runtime context, and waits for its completion signal when the presentation is blocking. Code must not manually duplicate editor-authored frames, positions, easing, sprite order, or timing. Rule code must not guess the duration of an authored animation.


Visual timing, staggering, easing, scale, offsets, curves, sound selection, and similar presentation values must be accessible from the editor. If a visual must be calculated procedurally, its important adjustable parameters must still be exposed through clearly named Inspector fields or presentation configuration assets.


The editor determines how a result looks and sounds. Authoritative rule code determines what the result is.

Any existing variable that materially affects design, balance, pacing, layout, presentation, or tutorial structure and may require adjustment must be prioritized for clear editor access rather than hardcoded inside implementation logic.

Use clear Inspector labels, grouping, tooltips, validation, and intentional defaults so important variables are understandable and safe to change without opening the implementing script

DECAY UNITY NAMING


GENERAL
- Project/title spelling: DECAY.
- C# namespace root: Decay.
- C# types and public members: PascalCase.
- Private fields: _camelCase.
- Method parameters and local variables: camelCase.
- Interfaces: IName, such as IRandomSource or IBattleCommand.
- Booleans begin with Is, Has, Can, Should, or Was when possible.
- Events describe completed facts in past tense or established state: DiceMoved, PhaseChanged, SlotBroken.
- Avoid unexplained abbreviations.
- Use Dice rather than Die always for clarity.




RESPONSIBILITY SUFFIXES
- Definition: immutable authored content, usually a ScriptableObject.
- Config: shared tuning or setup values.
- State: authoritative mutable runtime data with no presentation.
- Controller: accepts requests and coordinates a bounded system.
- Resolver: calculates an outcome without presenting it.
- Executor: performs an ordered action/command plan.
- Request: represents an attempted action that has not yet been approved.
- Gate: answers one specific permission question without changing state.
- Validator: evaluates validity when Gate is not the appropriate architectural term.
- Command: represents an approved operation.
- Fact: structured data describing a completed authoritative change.
- View: Unity scene/prefab presentation and input surface.
- Presenter: converts state/events into a view instruction when a separate object is useful.
- Repository or Catalog: resolves stable IDs to definitions.
- SaveData: serialized persistence representation.




Avoid using Manager for every object. A Manager name is allowed only when the object genuinely coordinates a broad lifecycle and no narrower responsibility name is clearer.




DICE TERMINOLOGY IN CODE
Use Dice consistently in type and member names, including when one gameplay object is being discussed, unless Elise later approves a different code convention. Examples: DiceDefinition, DiceRuntimeState, DiceView, selectedDice, activeDiceId, IReadOnlyList<DiceRuntimeState> dice.




The glossary remains the authority for communication terminology even if a future code-name exception is approved.




SLOT IDS
- Human/document label: 1E through 6E and 1P through 6P.
- Public/runtime identity: Side plus a one-based logical Number from 1 through 6.
- Internal arrays and lists use zero-based storage indexes from 0 through 5.
- Convert between SlotId.Number and a storage index only through one shared conversion helper.
- Gameplay code must not perform scattered manual +1 or -1 conversions.
- Never infer a slot from its GameObject name during play.

STABLE IDS
Persistent identity must not depend on display names, asset filenames, GameObject names, scene placement, or collection position.


Definition IDs identify authored content and use a lowercase category.name pattern:
- dice.clockwork
- dice.blessed
- charm.red_checker
- enemy.tutorial_dealer


Every owned Dice receives a mandatory automatically generated unique instance ID separate from its definition ID. Fields and properties that contain an identity end in Id, such as definitionId, instanceId, or slotId.




OPERATION TYPE NAMES
Names for gameplay operations must state what type of operation they represent. Do not use a generic root such as PhaseChange or DiceMove for several unrelated responsibilities.


Examples:
- Attempted action: MoveDiceRequest or PhaseChangeRequest.
- Permission check: DiceMovementGate or PhaseTransitionValidator.
- Approved operation: MoveDiceCommand or ChangePhaseCommand.
- Completed structured result: DiceMovedFact or PhaseChangedFact.
- Ordinary event raised after completion: DiceMoved or PhaseChanged.




ASSET NAMES
Use predictable descriptive names. Use prefixes to declare what TYPE of object or code is presented, then describe what it does or is attached to, and then any other details
Suggested patterns:
- Dice definition: def_DICE_Clockwork
- Charm definition: def_CHARM_RedChecker
- Enemy definition: def_ENEMY_TutorialDealer
- Battle config: conf_BATTLE_Tutorial_01
- Tutorial plan: plan_TUTORIAL_IntroBattle
- Prefab: PF_DiceView, PF_SlotView
- Animation clip: ANIM_Dice_Roll, ANIM_Slot_Decay
- Animator controller: AC_Dice, AC_Hourglass
- Sprite: SPR_Hourglass_Roll, SPR_Hourglass_Decay
- Material: M_Table_Unlit
- Sound: SFX_Dice_Roll, SFX_Slot_Break




GameObject names should describe scene role: Slot_1E, Slot_1P, DiceViewRoot, PlayerDrawer, EnemyDialogueAnchor.




SERIALIZED FIELDS
Prefer private serialized fields over public mutable fields:




[SerializeField] private DiceDefinition _definition;
[SerializeField] private Animator _animator;




Expose read-only properties or explicit methods when another object needs access.




C# FILE AND MEMBER NAMES
- A script filename must match its primary type name.
- Use one primary public type per file.
- Collections use plural names, such as dice, slotStates, or pendingCommands.
- Identity variables end in Id, such as definitionId, instanceId, or slotId.
- Methods use specific action names, such as RequestMove, ResolveRound, or PlayDecay.
- Do not use vague names such as HandleData, ProcessThing, DoAction, UpdateStuff, Helper, or Utils.
